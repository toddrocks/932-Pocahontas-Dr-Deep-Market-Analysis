01
The Approach

There is a version of this analysis that runs forty adjustments across a spreadsheet and produces a number to the dollar. It looks impressive and it hides a problem: every adjustment runs through the same handful of judgment calls, so if one is off, the whole thing is off in the same direction and nothing in the output warns you.

So this was built the opposite way. Four methods, each starting from a different question, each capable of producing a different answer.

They do share the same underlying market data. That is unavoidable and it should be said plainly. What differs is the question each one asks and the path it takes to an answer, which means an error in one does not automatically propagate to the others. Where they agree, the agreement is informative. Where they diverge, the spread tells you how much confidence the number deserves.

THE FOUR QUESTIONS
Where does this property sit among what has actually sold? What is the land worth, plus what sits on it? Where does it fall between the two sales that bracket it? And what do renovated homes on this water actually trade for per square foot?

THE DATA BEHIND IT
Sixteen Fort Walton Beach bayou waterfront properties over twenty four months: eleven closed sales, two pending, three active listings.

What Drives Waterfront Value Here

SIZE
Square footage stops paying above roughly 2,600 feet.
One recent sale added more than 3,000 square feet and a pool over a comparable property and gained almost nothing for it. At 2,347 square feet, your home is not penalized for its size. It sits right at the top of what this market pays for.

FRONTAGE
Waterfront feet are worth far more at the beginning than at the end.
The first fifty feet buys everything that matters: the water, the view, the dock, the address. Every foot after that returns less.

The market proved this twice in 2026. Two Cinco Bayou properties with roughly double your frontage sold for $825,000 and $775,000. Both are dated. Frontage is the one category where they beat you, and it was not enough to carry either of them within $250,000 of the price recommended here. Your 54 feet is real and it is accounted for. It is not what sets the price on this bayou.

Land carries the value.
Cinco Bayou frontage is finite. Your 0.85 acres, confirmed against county records, is larger than nearly every waterfront parcel that has sold here. The parcel runs 436 feet deep and widens toward the road, which places the home well back with mature tree cover on both sides. Buyers feel the privacy before they ever reach the water.

WATER ASSETS
The water is a set of assets on its own.
Of the comparable sales behind this analysis, one has a dock but no lift or boathouse, and one has no boating infrastructure at all. Your property has deep water, a permitted dock with boathouse and lift, and a sound seawall. Because Cinco Bayou sits outside any aquatic preserve and your dock structure is permitted and documented, that package transfers cleanly rather than becoming a question in underwriting.

It also carries a transferable flood policy at $2,677 a year, meaning the buyer can take over your existing policy rather than buying a new one at today's rates. As federal flood rates continue climbing, that is real money to a buyer every year they own the home. The prior listing never presented it.

The Market Since 2024

The Emerald Coast has softened modestly over the past two years. Waterfront has held up better than the broader market, which is what you would expect where supply is physically fixed.

The change is in leverage rather than price. In 2024, waterfront here regularly sold at or above asking. Nothing has sold above asking in 2026.

What has not changed is speed. Correctly priced waterfront still reaches contract in under sixty days. The three homes currently listed on Cinco Bayou have been on market between four and six months. They began at an average asking price of $1,519,667 and now average $1,166,667, a reduction of 23 percent. None is under contract.

METHOD TWO
What is the land worth, plus what sits on it?

The idea here is simple. Find three sales where the house adds very little, estimate what that house is worth, and subtract. What remains is the value of the land itself.

The rate applied to each house follows the condition of its finishes as recorded in the MLS, not its year built and not its sale price. A 1990 house with original kitchens, baths and flooring is rated below a 1980 house that has been repainted and reroofed, because a buyer pays for what is in front of them rather than for the date on the permit.

01
That interest came in a stronger market.
In 2024 waterfront here regularly sold at or above asking. That is no longer the case. The same buyer interest today supports a number below where it landed two years ago.

02

03
$1,100,000 is the tested top of this band. $1,189,000 is $89,000 above it.
Every active waterfront listing on Cinco Bayou priced at or above $1,100,000 is still on market, and the segment has never produced a sale above $1,250,000. $1,100,000 is the highest price point currently being worked by real buyers. Pricing $89,000 beyond it means asking the market to reach a number nothing has cleared, from a property carrying the narrowest frontage in the data.

04
The cost of testing a high price is not recoverable.
A listing draws its largest and most qualified audience in its first three weeks. Priced beyond the market, that audience passes and does not come back when the price is corrected later. That is exactly what happened in 2024, and the 122 days already on record make the risk sharper the second time, not softer.

The Strategy

PRICE
List at $1,100,000.
Thirty thousand dollars above the indicated value of $1,070,000, which is the negotiating room these transactions actually settle on. Level with the two listings at the top of the active band, arriving fresh against inventory buyers have already passed over. Visible to buyers searching in both directions from $1.1 million.

THE PRICE ADJUSTMENT PLAN
Every number is agreed before we go live.
Your frustration with the last listing was that the price sat still for four months. It will not happen again, and the way to guarantee that is to decide the triggers now, while nothing is at stake, rather than in a difficult conversation at day 60.

Day 14 review. Fewer than six showings and no written offer, the price moves to $1,075,000. This is a repositioning inside the same buyer pool, signaling movement to agents already watching the listing.

Day 30 review. Cumulative showings under twelve and no written offer, the price moves to $1,050,000. This one crosses a search filter threshold and opens a new pool of buyers who have not seen the property at all.

Day 60 review. If the property is still on market at day 60, price alone is no longer the explanation and we stop treating it as one. Everything gets re-examined: photography, remarks, the feature we lead with, the buyer segment we target, the feedback we have collected, and whether the price still fits what those sixty days have told us.

Two paths come out of that review. Rebuild and continue, with new photography, revised remarks, a repositioned campaign and a price set from what we have actually learned. Or withdraw and relaunch in spring.

The second path deserves to be understood now rather than in October. Withdrawing means off market from roughly late October until March or April. That is a full season, not a pause. The argument for it is not a technicality about the days-on-market counter, which the MLS may well carry forward regardless. It is that a property returning in spring with new photography, a new price and an audience that has not seen it in months competes far better than one that has been continuously visible for six months and has become part of the scenery.

Neither path is a failure. Accumulating days on market without a decision is.

No adjustment is automatic if the activity thresholds are met. Strong showing traffic without an offer signals a different problem than no traffic, and we address the actual problem rather than reflexively cutting.

APPRAISAL
The risk is real and it is worth naming.
When a buyer finances a purchase, the lender sends an appraiser to confirm the home is worth the contract price. This property carries the narrowest water frontage in the entire data set. An appraiser working mechanically will notice that first, and that appraisal is the most likely place a good contract comes apart.

Three things address it. The full document package goes to the appraiser at the same time it goes to the buyer, including the parcel map, the acreage verification, the dock permits and the renovation documentation. I provide the comparable sales and the reasoning behind them rather than leaving the selection to chance. And offers are evaluated on appraisal risk as well as price, which means a cash offer or a large down payment carries real weight against a marginally higher financed offer.

If an appraisal does come in below the contract price, the options are a buyer contribution of the difference, a renegotiated price, a second appraisal, or a formal challenge with supporting data. We decide which before it happens, not during.
